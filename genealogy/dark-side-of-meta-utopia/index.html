The Dark Side of Meta-Utopia: Hierarchy, World-State & Dystopia

The moral dangers of the many-worlds interpretation of the ideal civilisation design

by Max Ramsahoye

“The messiness of how a political apparatus is to be controlled and limited do not fit easily into one’s hopes for a sleek, simple [meta-]utopian scheme”

Abstract

In this article I critique the worldview of meta-utopianism – the vision of a pluralistic society containing multiple coexisting utopias rather than imposing a single moral framework – as fundamentally disingenuous and incoherent. I contend that meta-utopians are “closet authoritarians” who smuggle in liberal universalist assumptions while pretending neutrality, and further that their framework faces a problematic dilemma: either they have unstated narrow boundaries around acceptable plurality (revealing hidden authoritarianism), or they have no boundaries at all (leading to cultural relativism and moral abdication that preserves harmful ideologies). Additionally, I caution that over the long-term future, meta-utopianism operates as a covert form of cultural engineering under the guise of pluralistic values – a “Trojan horse” that achieves eliminationist goals through memetic manipulation rather than direct coercion. Ultimately, I warn that the civilisational commitment of meta-utopianism leads to preserving morally regressive societies and cultures (moral stagnation) under the guise of “enlightened tolerance”, resulting in a complicity from inaction (non-interference) against an ongoing moral (suffering) catastrophe.

  • This (2,000-word) post is a critical response to the emerging literature on Multi-Principal Pluralistic Alignment. See Archipelago and Atomic Communitarianism by Scott Alexander (2014) as a paradigmatic post.
  • This piece’s title is a subversive play on Nozick’s Anarchy, State & Utopia (1974), which coined the term ‘meta-utopia’.

Utopia is a meta-utopia: the environment in which Utopian experiments may be tried out; the environment in which people are free to do their own thing; the environment which must, to a great extent, be realized first if more particular Utopian visions are to be realized stably.

There will not be one kind of community existing and one kind of life led in utopia. Utopia will consist of utopias, of many different and divergent communities in which people lead different kinds of lives under different institutions. Some kinds of communities will be more attractive to most than others; communities will wax and wane. People will leave some for others or spend their whole lives in one. Utopia is a framework for utopias, a place where people are at liberty to join together voluntarily to pursue and attempt to realize their own vision of the good life in the ideal community but where no one can impose his own utopian vision upon others.

– Robert Nozick, Anarchy, State & Utopia

Meta-Utopia in the Age of ASI: Pluralistic Alignment

One vision of a post-ASI world-society is that it should be a meta-utopia and polytopia: a civilisation that is a container (the ‘meta’) for a plurality of utopias (the ‘poly’). Meta-utopia is a critical response to a classical utopia or mono-topia: a civilisation that universalises a single monolithic conception of the good society and the good life.

To bring ASI into this picture, parallel distinctions can be drawn between meta-alignment, poly-alignment (typically called ‘pluralistic alignment’) and mono-alignment. Whilst a mono-aligned ASI optimises for the ‘one true moral theory’, a meta-aligned and poly-aligned ASI would construct a global governance system and democratic deliberative process for multiple principals, axiologies and cultures to co-exist and engage with each other. In other words, an AI-enhanced variation of the ideal of the liberal international order.

The Dark Political Dynamics of Meta-Utopia

Before critiquing this view, I would first give the proponents of meta-utopia and meta-alignment their due: they are rightly responding to the risk posed by powerful AI that mirrors classical concerns over domination, state authoritarianism and cultural imperialism – motivated by the historical consciousness of actually-existing socialism in the 20th century and capitalist hegemony in the 21st. However, I am concerned they are swinging too far the other way and underappreciating the dark (political) dynamics (the ‘clash of civilisations’) of the meta-utopian vision.

Overall, I have noticed that meta-utopians are mistaken about the (self-perceived) morality of their (enlightened centrist) position.

Meta-Utopianism is Universalist

Firstly, they tend to express an aversion to ‘imposing a particular vision of individual or collective flourishing’ (Full-Stack Alignment, 2025). Whilst they may not like it, meta-utopians should be self-transparent and recognise that meta-utopia or meta-alignment is imposing a particular vision: it is a power-move in itself to realise a liberal pluralistic world-society over competing moralistic universalist visions. Meta-utopians may claim that they can realise this through diplomacy and democracy, but at the end of the day these mechanisms of interaction are themselves backed by the threat of force, and sustained by a balance of power with mono-utopians that necessitates a pragmatic compromise.

Meta-Utopianism is Naively Liberal-Democratic & Actually Machiavellian

Second, they tend to underplay that within a meta-utopia there may still be memetic dynamics and strategic gaming underneath the surface that determine which utopias rise and fall. Part of the freedom of the meta-utopian world entails that people can move between utopias and choose which one to live in. This would require an open information ecosystem where citizens of meta-utopia can be exposed to the range of utopias available to them. Whilst each utopia may purport to be in favour of mutual co-existence, some may be more universalist, and their belief in their own value may pose a risk of more subtle competition and conflict over minds/social psychology.

Meta-Utopianism is a Tyranny of the Tolerant & a Moral Stagnancy Risk

Thirdly, they tend not to demarcate exactly where the parameters of pluralism end – erring on the side of wider boundaries rather than narrower ones – and inadvertently descend into cultural relativism that allows any and all cultures to exist (aside from monopolistic ones), as the alternative would be coercive intervention against a culture to end it. This ‘Tyranny of the Tolerant or Multiplicity’ is the failure mode of meta-utopia in the same way that the ‘Tyranny of the Minority & Majority’ is the failure mode of mono-topia. The risk here is that a meta-utopia’s neutrality would preserve morally regressive cultures (e.g. fundamentalist religions) that are ‘trapped in history’, so to speak, and lock them in over the long-term future, resulting in moral stagnation (vs moral progress) and localised suffering catastrophes (vs existential flourishing).

Meta-Utopianism Begs the Question

This critique poses that meta-utopianism faces two pendular problems. The first is that meta-utopians have an unstated, particular conception of the parameters of plurality, such that (virtue-signalling) talk of plurality is already within a narrow frame of what is permissible. The second is that meta-utopians have no conception of the parameters of plurality, and thus, by refusing to realise a single vision, realise no vision at all – begging the question of what a good society is – and letting/legitimising whatever emerges (semi-anarchically). Pluralistic Alignment seems to beg the question on AI alignment, leveraging a ‘strategic ignorance’ of the complexities of moral philosophy to justify a form of liberal cultural relativism; the idea is that we don’t know what to align AI to, so we should instead align AI with the values of all groups and cultures (as if they are all equal, and as if there is no such thing as cultural evolution and moral progress, i.e. some belief and value systems that are better for people, populations and civilisation than others).

The Meta-Utopian Dilemma

Meta-utopianism becomes incoherent by trying to have it both ways on the crucial question of boundaries.

Dark 1: Pseudo-Liberal Authoritarianism

The first horn of the dilemma reveals meta-utopians as closet authoritarians: they actually do have implicit, narrow boundaries around what counts as legitimate plurality, but they refuse to acknowledge or defend these limits. When they speak of not ‘imposing a single vision’ and accommodating ‘diversity’, they’re already operating within a restricted liberal framework that excludes areas of human moral possibility – theocracies, ethnic nationalisms, revolutionary communisms, or any vision that rejects pluralism itself. Their supposed openness is actually a kind of false advertising that smuggles in substantive liberal commitments while pretending to be neutral about the good society.

Dark 2: Self-Defeating Pluralism

The second horn reveals meta-utopians as moral relativists: if their parameters are too wide and fail to make morally justified exclusions, then they end up in a world that is suboptimal. A meta-utopia that accommodates many separate visions realises no coherent vision at all. It becomes an empty container that stands for nothing because it tries to contain everything.

Both problems beg the question, because they avoid the fundamental task that any serious political philosophy must address: articulating and defending a conception of human flourishing. Meta-utopians either smuggle in their answer without transparency (first problem) or refuse to give a proper answer at all (second problem). Either way, they fail to engage honestly with the core question of what constitutes a good society, instead deferring to procedural rhetoric that abdicates their responsibility to seriously think through the real moral and political considerations of civilisation design.

Further Notes

It seems naively idealistic to me that goodness either won’t have to, or shouldn’t, be imposed/enforced against bad actors and worldviews that threaten the meta-utopian arrangement or would be perpetuated within it.

As far as I have seen, talk of pluralistic values is an ethical virtue-signal that lacks specificity as to the boundaries of pluralism, and begs the question of morality, shifting it from which morality should be actualised to which range of moralities should be permitted.

Meta-utopians are responding to the problem where the boundaries are narrow and good values are excluded. However, this creates a new problem, where the boundaries are too wide and bad values persist, with no attempt made to change them because of a libertarian non-interference principle. Here democratic open discourse is an underhanded form of memetic warfare that is presented as ideologically neutral when it is actually about changing minds towards a particular agenda.

The Immortal Meme or The End of Faith?*

* Derived from the New Atheism books by Dawkins & Harris.

As an anti-theist and rationalist, I imagine a non-impositional meta-utopian scenario where religions are allowed to co-exist and the goal is a peaceful, intergenerational transition to a world without religion through liberal, multi-cultural education.

However, this strategy to create moral progress without committing moral atrocities will be perceived by the religious of the world today as a subtle form of imposition, where, instead of making people lose their belief through coercion, the secular meta-utopian is manipulating the distribution and trajectory of memetics over long-term time horizons. The uncomfortable paradox of the secular meta-utopian is that this (possibly perceived) ideologically imperialist intervention would be morally good.

End of Faith? Example: Extended Analysis

Even as someone genuinely committed to avoiding direct coercion, I recognise that this “non-impositional” strategy is potentially a sophisticated form of cultural engineering designed to gradually eliminate religious worldviews from human civilisation. In this way, liberal, multi-cultural education is not a neutral institution, but a designed memetic weapon that systematically undermines traditional religious authority in favour of rationalist, secular values.

Religious communities are not being offered genuine coexistence, but a slow, painless extinction disguised as respect for their autonomy. In this way, the meta-utopian framework becomes a Trojan horse – it promises religious communities their utopian society to begin with, while gradually shaping its wider cultural environment in ways that threaten its future survival. Their children are systematically exposed to competing worldviews, critical thinking methods and Enlightenment values that criticise the worldview that religious life depends upon.

This is the Machiavellianism of meta-utopianism: it’s often more effective than direct persecution because it co-opts the language of tolerance and choice while achieving the same eliminationist goals. Religious parents can’t even properly resist, because doing so would mark them as “intolerant” within the meta-utopian framework, which operates as a kind of ideological laundering scheme – allowing secular progressives to pursue their transformative agenda while maintaining the moral high ground of appearing non-coercive, when in reality they’re just using more subtle, intergenerational methods of cultural influence.

The pragmatic meta-utopian faces the excruciating tension between (1) their procedural commitments to autonomy and (2) their substantive conviction that their vision would genuinely make everyone better off, including those who currently reject it. For instance, if it’s right that a secular, rationalist world would reduce suffering, increase human flourishing and eliminate the harmful effects of religious dogma, then the “manipulation” of the memetic environment is a moral cause.

This leads to a recognition that respecting people’s current preferences might actually be a form of moral cowardice. If religious worldviews genuinely trap people in cycles of guilt, limit their intellectual development, restrict their personal autonomy and perpetuate harmful social hierarchies, then allowing these systems to persist indefinitely in the name of “tolerance”, “diversity” and “autonomy” becomes complicity in ongoing harm. The children raised in fundamentalist communities who never get exposed to critical thinking or alternative possibilities – is their autonomy respected or denied?

The paradox intensifies because this logic can justify almost any form of ideological intervention. Every authoritarian believes their system would make people better off “if only they understood.” But the difference is that some visions might actually be objectively superior – and if they are, then the meta-utopian commitment to preserving inferior alternatives becomes not just naive but actively harmful. This is an uncomfortable recognition, because if religion does have a net negative impact, this “subtle imposition” could be the most ethical path available, even though it violates the very procedural principles that make the meta-utopian seem more enlightened than crude authoritarians.

Convergent Analysis from What We Owe the Future

This last point—that we need to structure global society so that cultural evolution guides the world towards better values and better societal structures—highlights an issue facing the design of a morally exploratory world that I’ll call the lock-in paradox. We need to lock in some institutions and ideas in order to prevent a more thoroughgoing lock-in of values. One challenge is that these institutions and ideas will be morally controversial; for example, from many fundamentalist religious perspectives, the idea that we would encourage or even allow a diversity of worldviews might be regarded as abominable. The lock-in paradox thus resembles the familiar paradox of tolerance—the necessity for liberal societies to defend themselves against intolerant views that would undermine their freedom, even if doing so requires curtailing the very tolerance they want to preserve.
