Literature Map of Meta-Utopia within the PARA-Sphere

Whilst the most prominent conceptual handle in political philosophy, meta-utopia has been reframed within the Progress (studies), AI alignment, Rationalist and Effective Altruist (PARA) sphere under new -topias and designations. Below I document the intellectual history of the eight most notable restylisations and elaborations of meta-utopia in the PARA-academic sphere.

Viatopia (MacAskill)

As mentioned previously, Viatopia is a higher-order conceptual engineering incorporating Nozick’s meta-utopia as one desideratum: within Viatopia, ‘many different moral points of view can flourish’. Whilst not directly attributed in the Viatopia and Better Futures Forethought materials, MacAskill does mention Nozick during a recent podcast with Liv Boeree:

❝ So philosopher Robert Nozick, calls Meta-utopia, Scott Alexander has a long blog post which calls it Archipelago and write a bit about a similar idea in What We Owe the Future [WWOTF]: you have many different societies and people can just create all sorts of different societies. As long as you’ve got freedom of entry and exit, then the hope would be that the best one kind of wins out or, at least, people get what they want from whatever societies they choose to enter into. ❞

In WWOTF, MacAskill refers to a version of meta-utopia characterised by a progressive (vs conservative) pluralism with the phrase a ‘morally exploratory world’:

❝ What we want to do is build a morally exploratory world: one structured so that, over time, the norms and institutions that are morally better are more likely to win out, leading us, over time, to converge on the best possible society… a morally exploratory world would favour political experimentalism—increasing cultural and intellectual diversity, if possible. We saw that we might already be on the way to a single global culture. If we are aiming to get to the best possible society, we should worry about premature convergence… ❞

Paretotopia (Drexler, Duettmann)

Premised on the conceptual cluster of Pareto improvements, efficiency and optimality, Paretotopia is an alternative frame developed primarily by the Foresight Institute, its co-founder Eric Drexler and president Allison Duettmann. The term originates in a 2018 EA Global talk by Drexler on Paretotopian Goal Alignment:

❝ For Paretotopian Goal Alignment, a key concept is Pareto-preferred futures, meaning futures that would be strongly approximately preferred by more or less everyone. If we can have futures like that, that are part of the agenda, that are being seriously discussed, that people are planning for, then perhaps we can get there. ❞

It was later written up as a Substack post of the same name at Drexler’s AI Prospects (2024), where he gives a more precise definition:

❝ The potential mitigation of zero-sum incentives in resource competition supports the broader concept of “Paretotopian outcomes” — accessible futures that would be strongly preferred by a wide range of actors today. These are outcomes where (almost) everyone is better off, and (almost) no one is worse off, compared to the current state. ❞

Whilst Drexler’s formulation is oriented more towards material conditions – as an existential risk and security factor – Duettmann’s later formulation in Gaming the Future (2025) broadens the concept towards cultural diversity:

❝ Value Diversity is Here to Stay – With diverse values that increasingly drift apart, we can’t rely on aligning players on one grand strategy. Instead, to set civilization up well over the next rounds of play, we must build a playing field that can handle fundamental value differences… [and] that allows for good games, as judged by each player… upholding voluntary cooperation could remain a stable common goal… to unlock Pareto-preferred worlds that are better for each by their standards. The rest of this book is about how to set civilization up for this path of intelligent voluntary cooperation ❞

Protopia (Crawford)

Another entry into the utopian landscape is ‘protopia’, originally coined by the futurist Kevin Kelly and later adopted by the progress studies thought leader Jason Crawford in The Techno-Humanist Manifesto (The Unlimited Horizon, part 2, chapter 8):

❝ I don’t believe in utopias. … I have not met a utopia I would even want to live in. … our destination is neither utopia nor dystopia nor status quo, but protopia. Protopia is a state that is better than today than yesterday, although it might be only a little better. ❞

– Kevin Kelly, Protopia, The Technium (2011)

Crawford brings in this excerpt from Kelly and then elaborates, in more poetic fashion, on the (more meta-utopian-esque) protopian possibilities of the ‘bold, ambitious future’ he envisions:

❝ The bold, ambitious future will not be a world of uniformity and sameness… It will be marvelously diverse, with room for every individual preference, aesthetic and lifestyle. The bold, ambitious future will not slow down. It will be far more fast-paced and exciting than anything humans have known… The bold, ambitious future is not a static Garden of Eden, but a dynamic wonderland, full of excitement, adventure, romance, exploration, curiosity, art, entertainment, play, and love. It is a paradise of human agency: the life we chose and the world we made. ❞

Core principles of protopia, like Paretotopia, include protecting the autonomy of the individual (voluntarism) and the diversity of culture (pluralism).

Stratified Utopia (Attubato)

Bringing meta-utopia into the space age, stratified utopia (like ‘strata’ in geology), coined by Carlo Attubato, envisions a multi-substrate architecture across space and time:

❝ mundane values get proximal resources (near Earth in space and time) and exotic values get distal resources (distant galaxies and far futures)… The cosmos gets divided into shells expanding outward from Earth-2025, like layers of an onion. Each shell is a stratum. The innermost shell Earth satisfies our most mundane values. Moving outward, each shell satisfies increasingly exotic values. ❞

Through compartmentalising the cosmos into curated ethical enclaves, stratified utopia satisfies both cosmic posthumanism – found in Bostrom’s Astronomical Waste – and terran humanism – found in Sagan’s Pale Blue Dot.

Atomic Communitarianism (Scott Alexander)

The closest rationalist parallel to Nozick’s meta-utopia is Scott Alexander’s Archipelago and Atomic Communitarianism (2014):

❝ Robert Nozick once proposed a similar idea as a libertarian utopia, and it’s easy to see why. UniGov does very very little… it just sits around preventing communities from using force against each other. That makes it very very easy for anyone who wants freedom to start a community that grants them the kind of freedom they want – or, more likely, to just start a community organized on purely libertarian principles. The United Government of Archipelago is the perfect minarchist night watchman state, and any additions you make over that are chosen by your own free will.…

other people could view the same plan as a conservative utopia. Conservativism, when it’s not just Libertarianism Lite, is about building strong cohesive communities of relatively similar people united around common values. Archipelago is obviously built to make this as easy as possible…

a better candidate for how to build a more Archipelagian world is to encourage the fracture of society into subcultures… At what point are national governments rendered mostly irrelevant compared to the norms and rules of the groups of which we are voluntary members? I don’t know, but I kind of look forward to finding out. It seems like a great way to start searching for utopia… the end result is that the closer you come to true freedom of association, the closer you get to a world where everyone is a member of more or less the community they deserve. That would be a pretty unprecedented bit of progress. ❞

Pluralistic Alignment (Audrey Tang)

Whilst introduced previously as a position advocated for by Iason Gabriel, pluralistic alignment is most passionately represented by the first digital minister of Taiwan, Audrey Tang. Their organisation the Plurality Institute has the motto to ‘cooperate across difference’, and their open-source book project Plurality (2024) dreams of ‘infinite diversity in infinite combinations’. The five principles of their mission statement further convey the essence of plurality:

❝ Plural technologies are… emergent from a diversity of systems of thought that facilitate the flourishing of and cooperation across a dynamic diversity of accountable and fluid social groups… Five principles summarize… pluralism:

  • Recognition: Social and intellectual structures should recognize diverse groups and networks, accord them status beyond the sum of members/individual ideas that make them up.
  • Subsidiarity: Decisions pertaining to activities and topics that primarily concern a particular group or network should be delegated to that group rather than structured or opined on by an external organization or jurisdiction, within the limits of requirements for protection of the rights of others.
  • Neutrality: We seek rough consensus across groups on a broad set of formalisms to mediate the relationship among those groups with a view to protection of their autonomy and mutual non-domination.
  • Cooperation across diversity: Actions and views that receive assent across a range of social groups or networks should be prioritized, even when holding fixed profiles of individual assent.
  • Adaptation: Groups are fluid and evolving. Social systems should anticipate and encourage such dynamism, fostering social recombination. ❞

The Moral Landscape (Sam Harris)

Harris is not particularly an EA intellectual, but rather an intellectual who is also an EA – he has taken the Giving What We Can pledge and has featured MacAskill, Ord, Bostrom and other EA thinkers on his podcast, Making Sense. Earlier in his philosophical career, Harris published The Moral Landscape, a book that attempts to ground secular objective morality (moral realism) in neuroscience, and visualises the possibility space of conscious states as a landscape of multiple ‘peaks’ and ‘valleys’ (moral pluralism):

❝ Throughout this book I make reference to a hypothetical space that I call “the moral landscape”—a space of real and potential outcomes whose peaks correspond to the heights of potential well-being and whose valleys represent the deepest possible suffering. Different ways of thinking and behaving—different cultural practices, ethical codes, modes of government, etc.—will translate into movements across this landscape and, therefore, into different degrees of human flourishing. I’m not suggesting that we will necessarily discover one right answer to every moral question or a single best way for human beings to live. Some questions may admit of many answers, each more or less equivalent. However, the existence of multiple peaks on the moral landscape does not make them any less real or worthy of discovery. Nor would it make the difference between being on a peak and being stuck deep in a valley any less clear or consequential. ❞

Walled Garden Utopias (Hanson)

Finally, Robin Hanson’s Utopia’s Garden, World, and Wall (2020) provides a tripartite imaginary for meta-utopian design, visualising each sub-utopia as a garden, the meta-utopia as a whole as the world, and the boundaries between sub-utopias as walls:

❝ Consider this in terms of “utopia” (or “heaven”). If it were possible, we’d all like to built and maintain utopias. And utopias have three key issue areas, which I’ll call “garden”, “world”, and “wall”.

Well inside each utopia is a “garden” full of rich detailed life, energy, color, aromas, passions, conflict, and play. Gardens give deep and even spiritual meaning and satisfaction, but depend crucially on complex opaque details of residents. Each utopia may have very different gardens, all full of life, but living very different lives. Making it hard to say much in general about how to manage gardens. Each utopia may have to mostly learn on its own here.

Utopias all exist in a larger “world”, a larger world that will try to coordinate in many ways, but will also fail to coordinate in as many ways. How exactly that grand coordination should be organized will always be a big political issue, on which many will compete fiercely to influence. Your utopia will want to play its part in this larger game, but can’t expect to have great influence there.

…Thus your utopia must have a “wall”, i.e., an interface between it and the world. You can’t presume that those you interact with out there share your utopian norms, culture, attitudes, or feelings of utopian solidarity. And if you try to make your interactions depend on the details of their norms, culture, etc., you’ll need to do that differently for each of the different kinds of others with which you interact.

There thus may be a place for thinking in general about utopian walls. About how your utopia’s precious garden might be better promoted via the right levels and types of interactions with outsiders. And instead of using a different type of interaction for each different type of outsider, it may work better to find good general ways that people from different utopias and sub-worlds can reliably and useful interact. That is, utopias may well want to have rather similar walls, even if they have very different gardens.

…I fully admit that it isn’t enough for a utopia to merely have good walls, or to sit in a good-enough world. It will also need good garden…

Which design issue re utopia interests you most? Its garden (internal relations), wall (how parts of it interface with outsiders), world (how it participates in larger scale governance/coordination, or founding (how it gets started)? ❞
