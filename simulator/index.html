Cultural Dynamics Simulator

A cellular automaton for topologically modelling civilisation-scale cultural dynamics across sovereign political units (‘the mechanics of meta-utopia’). A culture crosses a border when its cultural integrity overcomes the neighbour’s membrane impermeability, and then the neighbour’s own cultural integrity. Initially developed as a separate site.

The Culture Series

The simulator’s civilisations take their names from Iain M. Banks’s Culture series (1987–2012): the Culture itself, the Idirans of Consider Phlebas, the Azad of The Player of Games, the Gzilt of The Hydrogen Sonata, the Homomda, the Morthanveld, and the Sublimed, those who have left the material galaxy altogether. The Culture is a post-scarcity civilisation of humans, aliens and machines, run largely by vast artificial intelligences, the Minds, with no money, no laws to speak of, and nothing to stop anyone leaving: high cultural integrity behind open membranes.

It is also the series’ standing question for meta-utopia. Through Contact, and its covert arm Special Circumstances, the Culture intervenes in other civilisations, as it does with the Azad, convinced that it is helping. Whether that is uplift or a slow, benevolent takeover is the tension the simulator plays out, between a plurality of sub-topias and one culture strong enough to absorb the rest. Banks set out the Culture’s politics, economics and Minds in his essay A Few Notes on the Culture.

  1. Each hexagon is a sub-topia. Its fill is its culture; the number inside is its cultural integrity; the label beneath is its sub-topian status; its outline is its membrane, thicker and darker the more impermeable it is.

  2. A’s culture reaches B’s border. It crosses only if A’s cultural integrity (8.2) is higher than B’s membrane impermeability (5.1). It is: the membrane gives way.

  3. Inside, A’s culture meets B’s. It takes hold only if A’s cultural integrity (8.2) is also higher than B’s (3.5). It is: B cannot hold on to its own culture.

  4. B adopts A’s culture, and with it A’s sub-topian status, cultural integrity and membrane: B is now part of The Culture, utopian, with an integrity of 8.2 and a membrane of 4.0.

  5. Two sub-topias, one culture. On a full map the same two tests run between every pair of neighbours, turn after turn, until nothing more changes.

The Rule

Every sub-topia carries four things: a culture; a sub-topian status, whether it is utopian, notopian or dystopian; a cultural integrity, how strongly that culture holds and pulls; and a membrane impermeability, how closed its borders are. Only integrity and membrane decide whether a culture crosses, and its status travels with it. A culture crosses from one sub-topia into its neighbour only by passing two tests, in order. The tests run in both directions at once: A against B, and B against A.

Test 1

The membrane

integrityA > membraneB

Is the incoming culture’s cultural integrity higher than the neighbour’s membrane impermeability? If not, it is stopped at the border.

Test 2

The culture inside

integrityA > integrityB

Is it also higher than the neighbour’s own cultural integrity? If not, it gets in but cannot take hold.

Above, A’s integrity of 8.2 beats B’s membrane of 5.1, and then B’s integrity of 3.5, while B’s integrity of 3.5 cannot get through A’s membrane of 4.0. B is assimilated: it takes on A’s culture together with A’s status, integrity and membrane, and turns from dystopian to utopian.

Three Outcomes

Keep A as it is above, with a cultural integrity of 8.2 and a membrane of 4.0, and change its neighbour. Whether A is blocked, deflected or assimilates B depends on B’s two numbers: its membrane impermeability, then its own cultural integrity. Each card also tests B against A: a B with an integrity above 8.2 would pass both tests and take over A instead. In each case, the number in a hexagon is its cultural integrity.

From Two Sub-Topias to a Civilisation

The same two tests run between every pair of neighbours on a map, all at once, turn after turn, until no more cultures change. Where membranes are high everywhere, cultures stay sealed in place: enclosure. Where membranes are low and one culture’s integrity is far above the rest, it spreads from neighbour to neighbour: takeover. The typology of the four attractor states describes where each combination leads.

Three Layers of One Map

A sub-topia’s four properties can be read off a single hexagon, as above, or separated into layers, as in the Cultural Dynamics Simulator. Here one map of sixty-one sub-topias is drawn three times: by culture, by sub-topian status, and by cultural integrity, with each membrane drawn on it. When a sub-topia is assimilated it changes on all three layers at once, since it takes on the incoming culture’s status, integrity and membrane together. The layers show what a single view hides: where the strong and the weak cultures lie, and how closed each border is, as the map changes.

The Attractor States

Each state of the 2×2 is a different starting world for the same two tests. Below, nineteen sub-topias are set at the integrity and permeability of each state, and left to run: what each world becomes follows from the rule alone. Permeability is the inverse of membrane impermeability: a closed membrane is a high number.

Cultural Dynamics Simulator

Everything above, in one console. Choose a civilisational scenario, or start from a random world, and run it: the same two tests play out across a map of sub-topias, shown on three grids – culture, sub-topian status, and cultural integrity with each membrane drawn on it – while a marker traces where the civilisation sits on the four attractor states, turn by turn. When the map settles, the report below the grids sets out how the world changed.
